# Farmland Investing Guide: Row Crops vs Permanent Crops

Fractional farmland platforms offer exposure to two fundamentally different agricultural models: **row crops** (annuals like corn and soybeans) and **permanent crops** (orchards and vineyards). Understanding the differences is critical to matching investments with your risk tolerance and timeline.

## Row Crops: Annual Planting

### Examples
- Corn, soybeans, wheat, cotton, rice

### Economics
- **Planting**: Each spring (Northern Hemisphere)
- **Harvest**: Single season (fall typically)
- **Cash Flow**: Annual income from crop sales
- **Capital Intensity**: Lower (tractors, seed, fertilizer)
- **Flexibility**: Can rotate crops year-to-year

### Typical Returns
- **Cash Yield**: 3-6% annually
- **Appreciation**: 2-4% annually
- **Total**: 5-10% target IRR

### Risk Factors
- Commodity price volatility (corn, soy futures)
- Weather (drought, flooding, frost)
- Input cost inflation (fertilizer, diesel)
- Soil depletion over time

<Callout type="info">
Row crop farms benefit from **crop insurance**, which mitigates catastrophic loss but doesn't eliminate income volatility.
</Callout>

## Permanent Crops: Long-Term Investment

### Examples
- Almonds, pistachios, walnuts, wine grapes, citrus, apples

### Economics
- **Establishment**: 3-7 years before commercial production
- **Productive Life**: 20-40 years
- **Cash Flow**: Minimal for first 3-5 years, then strong
- **Capital Intensity**: High (irrigation, trellising, trees/vines)
- **Flexibility**: Cannot easily switch crops

### Typical Returns
- **Cash Yield**: 0-2% early years, 6-10% at maturity
- **Appreciation**: 3-6% annually
- **Total**: 8-14% target IRR (long-term)

### Risk Factors
- Water availability (drought, aquifer depletion)
- Tree/vine disease
- Long payback period (capital tied up)
- Market oversupply (e.g., almond glut)
- Climate shifts impacting growing zones

## Geographic Concentration

### Row Crops
- **Midwest**: Corn Belt (Iowa, Illinois, Indiana)
- **South**: Cotton, rice (Texas, Arkansas, Louisiana)
- **Plains**: Wheat (Kansas, Nebraska, Dakotas)

**Advantage**: Geographic diversification easier; multiple growing regions

### Permanent Crops
- **California**: Dominates U.S. tree nut and grape production (80%+)
- **Pacific NW**: Apples, pears (Washington)
- **Florida**: Citrus (declining due to disease)

**Risk**: Geographic concentration means water policy, state regulations, and local climate have outsized impact

## Cash Flow Comparison

### Row Crop Farm (Example: 1,000 Acres Iowa Corn/Soy)

- **Year 1**: 4% cash yield
- **Year 5**: 4.5% cash yield (assuming stable land value)
- **Year 10**: 5% cash yield

**Pattern**: Steady, predictable income from day one

### Permanent Crop Farm (Example: 100 Acres California Almonds)

- **Year 1-3**: 0% cash yield (trees establishing)
- **Year 4-5**: 2-3% cash yield (first commercial harvest)
- **Year 6-10**: 7-9% cash yield (mature production)
- **Year 10+**: 8-10% cash yield

**Pattern**: J-curve; negative/low early, strong later

<Callout type="warning">
If you need income today, permanent crop farms are the wrong choice. They require patient capital.
</Callout>

## Which Is Right for You?

### Choose Row Crops If:
- You want cash flow starting year one
- You prefer lower capital intensity
- You're risk-averse (crop insurance available)
- You have a shorter time horizon (3-5 years)
- You value geographic diversification

### Choose Permanent Crops If:
- You have a 10+ year investment horizon
- You're willing to forgo early income for higher long-term returns
- You believe in premium crop pricing power
- You're comfortable with California water risk
- You want asymmetric upside potential

## Tax Considerations

Both row and permanent crop farms typically issue **K-1s** and offer:

- Depreciation deductions (equipment, irrigation)
- Potential Section 179 expensing
- Long-term capital gains treatment on land appreciation

## Inflation Hedge Potential

Both farmland types hedge inflation, but through different mechanisms:

- **Row Crops**: Commodity prices often rise with input costs
- **Permanent Crops**: Premium products (organic almonds, fine wine) can pass through price increases

## Final Thoughts

There's no universally "better" choice—row crops and permanent crops serve different investor profiles:

- **Row crops** = Bond-like: steady income, lower volatility, shorter duration
- **Permanent crops** = Equity-like: growth, higher risk/return, long duration

Most sophisticated investors allocate to both for diversification within their farmland portfolio.
